Early Predynastic

Badarian · c. 4400–4000 BCE
7photos

The first farmers of Upper Egypt left no monuments, only their graves. Buried in shallow pits on the desert edge, laid on the left side with the head to the south, they took with them combs of bone and ivory, black-topped rippled pottery, and beads of shell and serpentinite. Much of what Egypt would later become is already implied here — the care for the dead, the reach for distant materials, the desert doing the preserving.

Naqada I

Amratian · c. 4000–3500 BCE
10photos

Farming villages along the river grew into something more stratified. Grave goods began to cluster, some burials markedly richer than their neighbours in the same cemetery — the earliest visible sign of a society sorting itself into ranks.

Naqada II

Gerzeh · c. 3500–3200 BCE
2photos

Naqada culture pushed north out of its Upper Egyptian heartland, carrying its pottery, its burial customs and its tastes down the length of the valley. Whether that was trade, migration or something more forceful is still argued. That it ended with a single material culture running from the cataracts to the Delta is not.

Naqada III · Dynasty 0

c. 3200–3100 BCE
7photos

Kings appear before Egypt does. In the century before unification, rulers at Abydos and Hierakonpolis were already using serekhs, ceremonial palettes and maceheads — the full vocabulary of pharaonic power, assembled before there was a pharaoh to wield it.

Early Dynastic

1st–2nd Dynasty · 3100–2686 BCE
34photos

With Narmer, Upper and Lower Egypt become one kingdom, and the state starts keeping records of itself. This is the largest group of objects on this page: palettes, ivory labels, seal impressions and the royal tombs at Abydos, where the machinery of divine kingship was worked out for the first time.

Old Kingdom

3rd–6th Dynasty · 2686–2181 BCE
10photos

The age of the great pyramids is also the age of the first named physicians. Egyptian medicine was already a profession with titles, instruments and specialisms — the swnw, working with poultices, blades and incantations in roughly equal measure.

Middle Kingdom

11th–13th Dynasty · 2055–1650 BCE
12photos

After a century of fracture Egypt reunified and turned inward, towards literature, administration and the body. The Kahun papyrus, the oldest surviving gynaecological text, comes from these years, separating conditions in language a modern clinician would recognise as diagnostic.

Second Intermediate

c. 1650–1550 BCE
3photos

Egypt divided again, ruled from the north by the Hyksos and from Thebes in the south. It is also when the Edwin Smith Papyrus was copied — a surgical text working through forty-eight cases from the head downwards, with almost no magic in it at all.

New Kingdom

18th–20th Dynasty · 1550–1077 BCE
33photos

Egypt at its widest reach, and its best documented. The Ebers and Hearst papyri, the honey harvest painted in Rekhmire's tomb, the bag of natron from Tutankhamun's embalming cache — the empire's wealth shows up in its pharmacy as much as in its temples.

Third Intermediate

c. 1077–664 BCE
1photo

Central authority fragmented between Tanis, Thebes and Libyan dynasts. Medicine stayed where it had always been safest: in the temples, in the hands of priests of Sekhmet, goddess of both plague and its cure.

Late Period

664–332 BCE
13photos

Egypt under Saite, Persian and native kings, self-consciously reaching back towards its own deep past. Bronze votives and faience amulets survive in enormous numbers from these centuries — Isis nursing Horus, Taweret, the wedjat eye — the everyday apparatus of protection.

Ptolemaic

332–30 BCE
4photos

Alexander's successors ruled Egypt in Greek while building temples in Egyptian. The papyri change language but not much else: a letter from the Fayum asks a man named Zenon for honey to treat an illness, much as an Egyptian physician would have done a thousand years earlier.

Roman Egypt

30 BCE – 641 CE
5photos

Egypt became Rome's granary, and its dead acquired faces. The Fayum mummy portraits — painted in encaustic on wooden panels, staring straight out at the viewer — belong to people who had their bodies wrapped in the old way and their likenesses painted in the new.

Egypt Today

modern photographs of the land, its sites and its materials
30photos

Not everything in a course about Egypt is an artifact. These are photographs of the land itself — the Nile at Aswan, the flood plain, the wheat, the nilometers cut into the rock at Elephantine — together with the modern maps and reproductions used to explain what the objects alone cannot show.
